# How Barcelona chooses its street trees {#sec:rules}

The method developed in this thesis does not choose trees in a vacuum. It works inside rules the city already applies. This chapter sets out those rules:

- the species cap and cover target of the 2017 master plan;
- the 2011 guide that links sidewalk width to tree size;
- an older replacement table that assigns species by district;
- the replacement mix announced in 2026.

It then places the cap in the literature on urban-forest diversity. The chapter ends with what the rules decide and what they leave open. That open part is where the method operates.

## The master plan: a 15% cap and a 30% cover target {#sec:rules-plan}

Barcelona's tree master plan, *Arbres per viure* [@ajuntament2017arbres], sets five targets for 2037. Two of them frame this thesis.

The first target is canopy cover. The plan aims to "increase tree cover in the city by 5%, reaching 30%" [@ajuntament2017arbres, §2.1; our translation, as for all quotes from Catalan and Spanish sources in this chapter]. Since the end point is 30%, the 5% reads as five percentage points. It prints no baseline figure, so the implied 2017 level of about 25% is a reading of the sentence, not a reported value.

The second target is diversity: within the urban fabric, no tree species should exceed 15% of the total [@ajuntament2017arbres, §2.1, pp. 24–25]. The rule is older than the plan. The 2011 street-tree management guide already set "a maximum of 15% of trees of the same species" as a management principle [@ajuntament2011gestio, p. 21], and repeats the limit on pp. 5 and 16. A project started in 2012 aimed to reach that balance within 50 years, around 2062 [@ajuntament2017arbres, pp. 20–21].

The cap applies city-wide. The plan counts about 201,600 municipal trees in the urban fabric, street rows and squares included [@ajuntament2017arbres, pp. 18–19]. No Barcelona document applies the 15% per neighbourhood or per street. The method's Porta-wide cap (Chapter 5) is therefore a local reading of a city-wide rule, and it is labelled as an assumption there.

The official documents give several reasons for the cap:

- the risk that one pest or disease removes a large share of a stock dominated by one species [@ajuntament2017arbres, §1.4.4; @ajuntament2011gestio, pp. 5, 16–17];
- resilience to climate change [@ajuntament2017arbres, §1.4.4, §2.1];
- biodiversity, including the fauna that trees support [@ajuntament2017arbres, §2.1; @ajuntament2011gestio, p. 16];
- a varied landscape with local identity [@ajuntament2011gestio, pp. 5, 21];
- fewer disservices such as allergenic, invasive or brittle species [@ajuntament2017arbres, §1.3.4, p. 14; @ajuntament2011gestio, p. 16].

None of them comes with a derivation of the number 15. The plane tree, *Platanus × acerifolia*, is the only species above the cap. The 2011 guide describes its dominance as a "position of vulnerability due to monoculture" [@ajuntament2011gestio, p. 17]. The guide also records the plane's pests, the lace bug *Corythucha ciliata* and powdery mildew, and its sudden summer leaf loss under water stress [@ajuntament2011gestio, pp. 37–38].

The share of planes depends on the denominator, and the sources do not agree on one (Table 3.1). Where the denominator is street trees (2010, 2022), the plane stood at 28–30%. The 2026 press figure of 27.45% is close, although its denominator is not stated.

| Year | Planes | Share | Denominator | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2010 | — | 30% | street trees | @ajuntament2011gestio [p. 17] |
| c. 2016 | c. 49,300 | 20.8% | all public trees, streets and parks | @ajuntament2017arbres [pp. 20–21] |
| 2022 | 42,709 | 27.7% | 154,235 street trees | press: @3cat2022 |
| 2026 | 43,722 | 27.45% | "trees of Barcelona" (not specified; ≈159,300 back-calculated) | press: @naciodigital2026 |

: Table 3.1. Share of plane trees in Barcelona's tree stock, by source.

In May 2026 the city announced that more than half of its planes, 56%, would be removed within ten years, bringing them down to 12% [@naciodigital2026; @beteve2026]. These figures come from the press, not from the plan, whose text contains neither the 12% nor a date for it. They are consistent with each other: 56% of 43,722 is about 24,500 trees, and if the total stays near 159,300 the remaining planes are 12.1% of it. The replacement is gradual: planes are replaced as they die and as more trees are planted [press: @beteve2026], and removed during street works [press: @3cat2022]. They are kept on large avenues and in emblematic places [@ajuntament2011gestio, p. 17].

## The 2011 guide: sidewalk width sets the tree size {#sec:rules-guide}

The 2017 plan states that the rules of the 2011 guide on species selection and planting remain in force [@ajuntament2017arbres, §1.4.3]. The guide's placement rule links each street's sidewalk width to a tree size class, defined by the adult crown and height (Table 3.2).

| Sidewalk width | Tree class | Adult crown / height | Spacing |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 2.5 m | avoid planting | — | — |
| < 3.5 m (narrow) | small | < 4 m / < 6 m | 7 m |
| 3.5–6 m (medium) | medium | 4–6 m / 6–15 m | 9 m |
| > 6 m (wide) | large | > 6 m / > 15 m | 13 m |

: Table 3.2. Sidewalk width and tree size class in the 2011 street-tree guide [@ajuntament2011gestio, pp. 22–23].

Further distances apply:

- no adult crown within 0.5 m of balconies or overhangs;
- more than 3 m from traffic lights and signs;
- more than 1 m from driveways [@ajuntament2011gestio, pp. 22–23].

The guide's species catalogue assigns each species a size class [@ajuntament2011gestio, pp. 49–56]:

- **large:** the plane, *Celtis australis*, *Tipuana tipu* and *Jacaranda mimosifolia*;
- **medium:** *Melia azedarach*, *Styphnolobium japonicum* and the evergreen *Brachychiton populneus*;
- **small:** *Pyrus calleryana* 'Chanticleer'.

The catalogue also marks each species with a coloured suitability code (plant, restricted, avoid, under study). The colours could not be read from the text of the document, so they are not used here.

This rule is the only quantitative placement rule in the documents read for this thesis, and the method enforces it per position (Chapter 5). It works on size alone. It says which size of tree fits a sidewalk, not which species of that size should go there.

## Gate 1: a rule for which species goes where {#sec:rules-gate1}

The tutor asked whether Barcelona already decides, by a stated criterion, which species replaces which tree and where. Two documents of Parcs i Jardins, the city's parks department, answer part of the question [@parcsijardins2004seleccio; @parcsijardins2005pla]. The first, *Arbrat viari: selecció d'espècies*, dates from 2002, revised in 2004, and its replacement table is marked for the department's internal use. The second is the 2005 street-tree management plan.

Both contain the same table for replacing dead trees, organised by district and sidewalk width [@parcsijardins2004seleccio, §2.4.2.1, p. 11; @parcsijardins2005pla, §3.4.3, p. 10]. For Nou Barris, the district that contains Porta, it reads:

- **narrow sidewalks:** *Prunus cerasifera* 'Atropurpurea';
- **medium sidewalks:** *Melia azedarach* or *Jacaranda mimosifolia*;
- **wide sidewalks:** keep the plane or the existing species.

The documents keep the plane only on large avenues, on exceptional streets with low, airy, sunny buildings, and where its growth does not interfere with urban activity. They give a priority order for replacing a plane:

1. road-safety problems;
2. a dead tree;
3. an aesthetically unrecoverable or declining tree;
4. unifying a stretch where another species already dominates;
5. taking advantage of street works [@parcsijardins2004seleccio, §2.4.2; @parcsijardins2005pla, §3.4.2].

Rebuilt streets follow a broader list, with no district, drawn "from the experience of the technicians". Exceptions keep a species where conversion has already started [@parcsijardins2004seleccio, §2.4.2.2–2.4.3].

The criteria the documents name are tolerance of local conditions, urban typology, tradition and earlier plantings [@parcsijardins2004seleccio, §2.4.2]. Shade, heat and runoff are not among them.

The 2011 guide no longer prints the table. It says only that a dead tree is replaced by the same species or a new one "taking into account the species catalogue" [@ajuntament2011gestio, p. 28]. In 2022 the city said it had decided which species can replace dying trees, using street orientation, aerial space, soil quality and desired shade [press: @timeout2022]. No document with that list was found.

**Does Porta follow the table?** Planting dates exist for 559 of Porta's 2,825 inventory trees (2007–2026). Of these, 368 stand on a sidewalk whose width class could be measured (Chapter 5).

- **Narrow sidewalks:** 6 of the 58 trees planted (10%) are *Prunus cerasifera*. All six are recorded as 'Pissardii' rather than the table's 'Atropurpurea'; the match ignores the cultivar. The most common species is *Pyrus calleryana*, with 30 trees.
- **Medium sidewalks:** 11 of 112 (10%) match, all of them Melia, none Jacaranda. Brachychiton leads, with 26 trees.
- **Wide sidewalks:** 11 of 198 are planes, 2 of them the PLATANOR cultivar, whose status as a plane is disputed. The other 187 belong to 23 other taxa.

The table allows "the existing species" on wide sidewalks, and the inventory does not record what each new tree replaced. The wide class therefore cannot be judged.

These figures are an upper bound on non-compliance, not a measure of it. The table covers dead trees only. Rebuilt streets and exceptions follow other lists, and the inventory cannot separate the three cases. Peaks of 70 plantings in 2010 and 102 in 2025 suggest street works.

The answer to gate 1 is therefore mixed:

- **A rule exists.** A structured rule for which species goes where has existed since at least 2004.
- **It is qualitative.** It rests on tradition and the technicians' experience.
- **It is not visible in practice.** It is not printed in the guide now in force, and in Porta up to about 90% of dated plantings on narrow and medium sidewalks do not match it.
- **It quantifies nothing.** No version of it estimates heat or runoff.

## The 2026 replacement mix and the baseline of this thesis {#sec:rules-mix}

The most recent statement of what replaces the planes is a target mix reported in May 2026 [press: @beteve2026]:

- planes and Celtis at about 12%;
- Melia at 7.25%;
- "around 5%" for species such as *Pyrus calleryana*, *Styphnolobium japonicum* and *Tipuana tipu*.

The article does not say whether the 12% applies to each of the first two species or to both together, and the same ambiguity holds for the 5%. Reading it as "together" would leave Celtis near zero, because the planes alone are to reach 12%. "Each" is the plausible reading. Either way, Celtis must fall from 13.8% of street trees (in the city inventory snapshot used in this thesis), so it can absorb few replacements.

The thesis compares its method against two possible versions of the city's own rule:

- **S1a:** the 2026 mix, placed at random within the 2011 sizing rule. The tool reads the figures as "each" and uses them as relative weights (12 : 7.25 : 5 : 5 : 5), which is an assumption.
- **S1b:** the 2004/2005 Nou Barris table.

S1a is the baseline, decided on 24 October 2026. It is the current policy, while the table is older and no longer printed. S1b is cited here as the documented older rule and is not run. If Parcs i Jardins publishes a current per-street list, that list replaces both. Because the 2026 mix comes from the press, the baseline carries that uncertainty, and the results in Chapter 7 state it.

## The cap in the diversity literature {#sec:rules-diversity}

Barcelona's 15% has a lineage in the literature on urban-forest diversity:

- Barker (1975, as cited in Kendal et al., 2014) proposed 5% per species, a target later judged unachievable.
- Later benchmarks clustered at 10–15% per species [Grey & Deneke, 1986; Miller & Miller, 1991; Raupp et al., 2006, all as cited in @kendal2014; @santamour1990].

The best-known version is Santamour's 10-20-30 rule: no more than 10% of any species, 20% of any genus and 30% of any family [@santamour1990, p. 64]. Santamour himself did not know who first proposed the 10% rule [@santamour1990, p. 58]. His rationale matches Barcelona's: protection against pests and diseases, with Dutch elm disease and chestnut blight as the cautionary cases.

Santamour also argues that a limit on species is not enough. Pests tend to follow genera and families, so a rule on species alone "offers little protection", and genera and families must be limited too [@santamour1990, pp. 62–63]. Barcelona caps species only.

Two of his examples bear on the species this thesis considers:

- sycamore anthracnose, a disease of *Platanus*;
- fire blight, to which genera of the rose family, including *Pyrus*, are "potentially susceptible" [@santamour1990, pp. 58, 63].

Within the thesis's seven-species palette, Tipuana and *Styphnolobium* share a family, the legumes (Fabaceae). A family-level cap would bind them together. The method therefore reports every layout's largest genus and family share against Santamour's 20% and 30%, without imposing them, since the city does not (Chapter 5).

Kendal et al. [-@kendal2014] tested such benchmarks against 151 tree inventories from 108 cities:

- **Species:** in every climate group, the most common species is far above 10%. Santamour's 10% sits near the global 10th percentile.
- **Genus and family:** the median shares are close to 20% and 30%.
- **Streets vs parks:** streetscapes are less diverse than parks; the most common genus is 24% on streets against 15% in parks. The authors suggest higher benchmarks for streets.

Their warning applies directly to Barcelona. Targets set "substantially lower than existing relative abundance patterns" risk that "well adapted species may be abandoned for underperforming species", or that "changing species may lead to unexpected changes in the provision of ecosystem services" [@kendal2014, p. 415]. Barcelona's planes stand at about 27% of street trees against a 15% cap and a 12% target.

Two caveats limit the comparison:

- Kendal et al. write their sharper version of the warning, about "a reduction in the ecosystem service benefits", for streetscapes in Continental climates [@kendal2014, p. 415].
- For Temperate climates, and for urban forests across multiple land uses, they suggest benchmarks may even need lowering [@kendal2014, p. 416]. Barcelona's climate (Köppen Csa) falls in that group by our classification; Kendal et al. do not classify Barcelona.

The 15% is also the regional convention:

- the Diputació de Barcelona's street-tree guide names "a reasonable 15%" for one species across a municipality's whole inventory, as a non-binding reference [@diba2012];
- L'Hospitalet de Llobregat's approved green plan sets a 15% maximum [@hospitalet2019];
- Girona's 2025 plan is stricter, with 5-10-15 city-wide [@girona2025].

## What the rules decide and what they leave open {#sec:rules-gap}

Taken together, the rules decide a great deal about each replacement:

- **how many:** no species above 15% city-wide, and planes down to about 12%;
- **how large:** the size class set by sidewalk width;
- **from which list:** the catalogue, and the 2026 mix as reported.

Since 2004 they have also contained, on paper, a district table of which species goes where.

They do not quantify what a choice does. No city document estimates how a species at a given position changes pedestrian heat stress or storm runoff. The 2011 guide asks only, qualitatively, for shade trees that feel cool in summer [@ajuntament2011gestio, p. 16]. The criteria the older table names are tolerance, typology, tradition and experience.

The diversity literature reaches the same point from the other side. Caps such as 15% are rules of thumb without an empirical derivation [@santamour1990; @kendal2014]. Kendal et al. warn that cutting a dominant species can cost ecosystem services.

What is missing, in our reading, is performance data per species and per position that can be used inside the rules. The rules then stay as constraints, and the choice among the species they allow becomes measurable.

That is the role of the method in Chapters 5 to 7. Before it, Chapter 4 reviews the computational tools that come closest to this task.
