### 1. Who and what
**Say (64 words):** "Imagine you are a landscape designer who has to redesign a street in Barcelona. The layout of the street is already decided, so what is left to you is the trees: you have to decide which ones to keep, which to move or replace, and where to add new ones, so that the street becomes cooler in summer without getting worse when it rains."

### 2. Context
**Say (75 words):** "This is not a rare case. Since 2018, almost eleven thousand street trees in Barcelona have been replaced with a different species, one at a time. The city caps every species at fifteen percent, but it says nothing about which species should go where. And yet the choice matters, because in my site, Porta, trees with the same trunk size carry crowns that range from a full plane tree down to a quarter of one."

### 3. State of the art and gap
**Say (68 words):** "In the literature, the pieces already exist, but separately. Some models assess heat and runoff together, but on fixed scenarios, without choosing the species. Other tools rank or place species, but they look at only one service, or they ignore positions. Of the eighteen tools I reviewed, none picks the species tree by tree while respecting a city's own rules, and this is the gap my method addresses."

### 4. Refined question
**Say (68 words):** "My question started from trees against floods in Bologna. The feedback narrowed it, because runoff depends more on the tree pit than on the species, so runoff became a check that the design does no harm. The question is now: to what extent can a method that chooses species and positions inside the city's rules give more summer cooling than the city's own practice, without losing rain interception?"

### 5. Fixed and free
**Say (70 words):** "Some things are fixed: the street layout and the city's rules, such as the fifteen percent cap, the sidewalk width that decides the tree size, and the distance from lamps and signs. What is free are four actions for every tree: keep it, move it, replace it, or add a new one, but never remove a tree without replacing it. The tool advises, while the designer keeps the final decision."

### 6. Method
**Say (68 words):** "The method starts with a screening that runs once for every tree and every free spot. It records what stands there, where it is, and what each of thirty species would do in that spot, in terms of summer shade, winter shade and rain held. An exact optimiser then chooses the best combination within the rules, and the result is compared with today and with the city's practice."

### 7. Why this way, and not another
**Say (86 words):** "I did not choose field measurements, because a layout that does not exist yet cannot be measured, and I did not choose machine learning, because there is no outcome data to train it on. An exact optimiser fits because the city's rules are hard limits, and it can prove that its layout is the best one. The baseline is real, since it is what the city actually planted, and it also shows that one in five of its new trees is larger than its sidewalk allows."

### 8. First evidence
**Say (79 words):** "As a first test, I took twenty-six groups of trees that the city had really replaced and kept the same positions. With the city's choice, each tree gave a median of fifty-nine square-metre hours of summer shade, while with the method it gave one hundred thirty-nine, and the method came out ahead in twenty-two groups out of twenty-six. The next steps are to check heat with Ladybug and Infrared City, and to test the method in Mataró and Florence."
